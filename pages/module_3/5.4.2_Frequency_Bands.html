5.4.2 Frequency Bands

Channels

Each frequency band is divided into channels, which act as lanes within the band and allow multiple wireless networks to operate without interfering with each other. Channel width is determined by the band and AP configuration. Some standards support channel bonding (combining channels for greater width), which increases throughput.

Wi-Fi Frequency Band Comparison

Band Nominal Indoor Range Penetration Channels Speed Interference Risk
2.4 GHz 45 m (150 ft) Best; penetrates solid surfaces well Few; often congested Lowest High: Bluetooth, other Wi-Fi, microwave ovens
5 GHz 30 m (100 ft) Moderate; less effective than 2.4 GHz More channels; less congestion Higher than 2.4 GHz Lower than 2.4 GHz
6 GHz 15 m (50 ft) Weakest; least effective at penetrating surfaces Most channels; newest band, least congestion Highest Lowest; newer band with fewer competing devices

Note: clients are only likely to connect at full speed from one-third to one-half of the nominal ranges listed, depending on building features, interference, and the wireless standard in use. Power output is also regulated, which limits range.

Key Terms

Frequency band
A range of radio frequencies used by wireless networks. The three main Wi-Fi bands are 2.4 GHz, 5 GHz, and 6 GHz.
Channel
A smaller frequency range within a band that acts as a lane for a wireless network. Multiple channels allow multiple networks to share the same band without interfering.
Channel bonding
Combining adjacent channels to create a wider channel, increasing the amount of data that can be transmitted at once.
