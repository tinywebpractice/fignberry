7.3.7 Lesson Review — Q&A

Question 1

After several new computers are connected to a network device, every computer connected to the device begins to experience slow transfer speeds. Which of the following is the FIRST step to troubleshoot this problem?

  • Determine whether the network device is a hub or a switch.
  • Use a loopback plug to determine whether each port on the network device is good.
  • Use a cable tester to determine whether all cables between each computer and the device are good.
  • Use a loopback plug to determine whether the NIC in each computer is good.

The problem affects all connected computers, pointing to the device itself. A hub shares bandwidth among all devices, so adding more computers degrades performance for everyone. Identifying the device type is the logical first step before any cable or port testing.

Question 2

You have just installed a wireless network, but the network is experiencing slow speeds, especially when accessing the internet from your workstation. Which action should you try FIRST to resolve the issue?

  • Move your workstation closer to the wireless access point.
  • Add a network extender.
  • Move the wireless access point.
  • Reset your wireless router to the factory defaults.

Moving the AP is the easiest and least expensive first step. Moving the workstation could involve significant effort or may not be an option. A network extender is valid but adds cost. Factory reset could cause other issues and is unlikely to resolve slow speeds.

Question 3

A user reports intermittent connectivity and slow transfer speeds on the office Wi-Fi network. Upon investigation, you find that the user's device is connected to the 2.4 GHz band, while other devices connected to the 5 GHz band are not experiencing any issues. Additionally, the Wi-Fi analyzer shows that the 2.4 GHz band is heavily congested with multiple overlapping networks. What is the BEST course of action to resolve the issue?

  • Adjust the channel settings on the wireless access point to a less congested channel in the 2.4 GHz band.
  • Move the user's device closer to the wireless access point to improve the signal strength.
  • Replace the wireless access point with a newer model that supports higher speeds.
  • Configure the user's device to connect to the 5 GHz band instead of the 2.4 GHz band.

The 2.4 GHz band is confirmed congested by the analyzer, and 5 GHz devices are unaffected. Switching the user's device to 5 GHz directly eliminates the interference source. Adjusting channels within 2.4 GHz provides limited relief given heavy congestion. Proximity and hardware replacement do not address the root cause.

Question 4

A user reports limited connectivity on their workstation. Upon investigation, you find that the workstation is connected to the network but cannot access certain external websites. Other users on the same network do not have this issue. You run the ipconfig command and notice that the workstation has a valid IP address, subnet mask, and default gateway. What is the MOST likely cause of the issue, and what should you do next?

  • The DNS server settings are incorrect. Reconfigure the workstation to use the correct DNS server.
  • The user's computer has a malware infection. Disconnect the workstation and run a malware scan.
  • The switch port is experiencing port flapping. Check the switch logs for port status changes.
  • The network cable is faulty. Replace the cable with a known good one.

Valid IP/subnet/gateway means Layer 3 routing is functional. Inability to reach external websites by name on a single host points to DNS misconfiguration. Port flapping and faulty cables would cause no/intermittent connectivity, not selective website failures.

Question 5

A user reports that their file transfers over the network are significantly slower than usual. Upon investigation, you find that the issue is isolated to this user's workstation. You check the cabling and find no visible damage or interference. What should you do next to troubleshoot the issue?

  • Reposition the user's workstation closer to the router.
  • Check the duplex settings on the user's network adapter and the switch port.
  • Replace the user's Ethernet cable with a shielded cable.
  • Update the firmware on the router.

Cabling is cleared; issue is single-host. The next structured step is checking for a speed/duplex mismatch between the NIC and switch port, both of which should be set to autonegotiate. Repositioning is a wireless fix. Shielded cable addresses interference that was not found. Router firmware affects all users, not just one.

Question 6

What is the maximum one-way latency that VoIP can support without significantly impacting call quality?

  • 500 milliseconds (ms)
  • 300 milliseconds (ms)
  • 50 milliseconds (ms)
  • 150 milliseconds (ms)

VoIP supports a maximum one-way latency of ~150 ms. 500 ms and 300 ms far exceed the threshold; 50 ms is too conservative.

Question 7

A network technician troubleshoots an issue where the video conferencing system keeps freezing up and dropping out. The technician determines that the network is not congested and the signal is not being processed fast enough to meet the streaming requirement. What is this type of issue known as?

  • QoS
  • Jitter
  • Latency
  • VoIP

Signal not being processed fast enough describes latency: the delay in data reaching the recipient. Jitter is variation in delay between packets and is typically caused by congestion, which is ruled out here. QoS is a fix mechanism, not a problem type. VoIP is a technology category.

Question 8

A company is experiencing poor VoIP call quality, including jitter and delays. You analyze the network and find that the issue occurs during peak usage times when multiple devices are streaming videos and downloading large files. The company uses a SOHO router without advanced traffic management features. What is the MOST likely cause of the VoIP issues?

  • The VoIP application is not compatible with the SOHO router.
  • The VoIP phones are outdated and cannot handle modern network traffic.
  • The SOHO router lacks Quality of Service (QoS) configuration to prioritize VoIP traffic.
  • The Internet Service Provider (ISP) is throttling VoIP traffic during peak hours.

Competing bursty traffic (streaming, downloads) floods the network during peak times. Without QoS, the router treats all traffic equally, causing VoIP packets to be delayed. The scenario identifies internal congestion and lack of traffic management as the key factors.

Question 9

You are an IT technician tasked with resolving a wired connectivity issue for a workstation in an office. The user reports intermittent connectivity problems. Upon inspection, you notice that the link LEDs on the network adapter and switch port are flickering inconsistently. What is the BEST next step to troubleshoot and resolve the issue?

  • Use a loopback tool to test the switch port.
  • Substitute the current patch cord with a known good cable.
  • Update the NIC's device driver software.
  • Replace the network adapter (NIC) on the workstation.

Inconsistently flickering LEDs indicate an unstable physical link. Step 1 of wired troubleshooting is always to check and substitute the patch cord. Loopback testing (Step 2), driver updates (Step 5), and NIC replacement all come later in the process.

Question 10

What is the first step in troubleshooting a limited connectivity issue for a single host in a wired network?

  • Update the NIC's device driver software.
  • Use a cable tester to verify the structured cabling.
  • Verify that the patch cords are properly terminated and connected to the network ports.
  • Check the switch configuration for port security settings.

Step 1 is always to check patch cord termination and connection. Structured cabling testing is Step 4; driver updates are Step 5; port security is a secondary consideration when substituting hosts.
