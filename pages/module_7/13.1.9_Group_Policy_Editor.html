13.1.9 Group Policy Editor

Overview

GUI tools like Settings and Control Panel modify registry-backed configurations, but many registry settings are not accessible through those tools. The Group Policy Editor provides a structured way to configure Windows settings without directly editing the registry. Vendors can also supply administrative templates to configure third-party software via policies.

Tool Scope Run Command
Local Group Policy Editor All settings for the local computer (Computer Configuration + User Configuration) gpedit.msc
Local Security Policy Editor Security settings only for the local computer secpol.msc
Group Policy Management (domain) Centrally manage GPOs across an entire Active Directory domain/OU structure GPMC (Group Policy Management Console)

Domain Group Policy (Active Directory)

  • Domain GPOs are created and managed in the Group Policy Management Console and stored as Group Policy Objects (GPOs) in Active Directory.
  • GPOs can be linked to the domain root (applies to all computers/users) or to specific Organizational Units (OUs) to scope them to a department or group.
  • The Default Domain Policy exists in every Active Directory environment and applies to all computers and users in the domain. The password policy is commonly configured here. Avoid modifying it for other purposes.
  • Policies can be triggered by events (user sign-in, startup) and are applied at boot and logon, and refreshed periodically in the background.

Policy States and Best Practices

  • Most policies have three states: Enabled, Disabled, and Not Defined. Some require a discrete value. Read each policy description carefully before changing it.
  • Always document policy changes. Troubleshooting the source of an unexpected setting change across many linked GPOs is a common and difficult IT task.
  • In large networks, Group Policy avoids the need to manually configure individual machines. One GPO change can affect hundreds or thousands of computers.
